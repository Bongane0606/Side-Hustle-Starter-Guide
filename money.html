Money plan

Save first.
Buy slower.
Grow cleaner.

A startup does not need fancy debt on day one. It needs a clear target, controlled spending, customer deposits, and records that show whether the business is improving.

Step by step

How to save for startup costs

  1. Write your minimum startup list

    Only include what you need to make the first paid sale. Leave out upgrades, decorations, branded clothing, and extra stock until customers prove they will pay.

  2. Add a safety amount

    Add 10% to 20% for transport changes, price changes, forgotten small items, cleaning supplies, airtime, and replacements.

  3. Choose a weekly saving target

    Divide the startup target by the number of weeks before launch. If the weekly amount is too high, reduce the startup list or launch with a smaller package.

  4. Keep startup money separate

    Use a separate envelope, account, or savings pocket. Do not mix business startup money with lunch money, transport money, or family spending.

  5. Use deposits to reduce risk

    If a customer booking forces you to buy stock, ingredients, or decor items, take a deposit first. Deposits are not profit; they protect the booking.

Simple rule

Do not buy expensive equipment until it will either earn money immediately or save money every month.

Borrow, rent, or partner first. Buy only after repeated bookings show the item will pay for itself.

After launch

What to do with first profit

  1. Replace used stock first

    Before spending profit, put back the money needed for the next order or booking.

  2. Pay yourself something small

    Even a small owner payment teaches you that the business must reward your time, not only buy more stock.

  3. Reinvest into one useful upgrade

    Buy the item that removes a real bottleneck: a better chair, better containers, extra glasses, a backdrop stand, or more worksheets.

  4. Keep a damage or emergency fund

    Decor items break, ingredients spoil, clients cancel, and transport fails. Keep some profit aside for recovery.

Beginner warning

Revenue is not profit. If a customer pays R1,000 and the booking costs R700 to deliver, the business did not make R1,000. It made R300 before other monthly costs.
